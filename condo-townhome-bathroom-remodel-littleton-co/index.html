The short answer

Remodeling a bathroom in a Littleton condo or townhome takes three approvals: your HOA's written OK under its declaration and rules, a building permit from the city or county for plumbing work, and a plan for shared water shutoffs. If the change is for a disability, federal fair housing guidance says the association cannot unreasonably refuse it.

That last point surprises many owners. A tub-to-shower conversion for someone who can no longer climb into a tub is usually treated as a "reasonable modification" under the Fair Housing Act, and the U.S. Departments of Housing and Urban Development and Justice have spelled out what an association may and may not ask for. We walk through it below, after the practical part: what to send the board, and how to keep the neighbors' water running.

The order of operations

What approvals does a condo bathroom remodel need?

Do them in this order and nothing waits on anything else.

Start with the declaration. Every Colorado condo or townhome community has recorded documents that define where your unit ends and the common elements begin. In many condos the drywall, fixtures and the pipes that serve only your unit belong to you, while the main stacks, shared walls and the slab belong to the association. In many townhome communities the owner holds more of the structure. The documents decide, not habit, so read them or ask management for the relevant pages before you design anything that cuts into a floor or a shared wall.

Then send a written request. Most associations want a short description of the work, the products, the contractor's name, license and insurance certificate, and the dates. A one-page summary with the itemized quote attached tends to move fastest. Ask how long the board takes to respond and whether an architectural committee reviews interior work at all; some only care about noise, hours and parking.

The permit comes next. Inside City of Littleton limits, plumbing changes need a permit and contractors must be licensed with the city. Many condo complexes with a Littleton address sit in unincorporated Arapahoe or Jefferson County instead. Our permit guide shows which office is yours, and our contractor checklist covers the license and insurance checks.

Accessibility

Can an HOA say no to an accessible shower?

Not without a good reason, if the change is needed because of a disability.

The 2008 joint statement from HUD and the Justice Department describes a reasonable modification as a structural change to existing premises that a person with a disability needs to fully use the home, and it lists "installing grab bars in bathrooms" as a typical example. It says the Fair Housing Act makes it unlawful for "a housing provider or homeowners' association to refuse to allow a reasonable modification" when it may be necessary, and it notes that courts have applied the Act to homeowners and condominium associations.

The same statement draws clear lines on both sides:

In practice, a calm written request that explains the need, with a note from a doctor or therapist if you have one, the itemized quote and the contractor's license and insurance, resolves almost every case. Many associations already ask every contractor for a certificate of insurance on any job; that is the contractor's own coverage and is normal. This page is general information, not legal advice. Shared buildings

How do you remodel without shutting off the neighbors' water?

In a stacked condo, the bathroom above yours and the one below often share the same supply risers and drain stack. Before install day, ask management three questions: does my unit have its own shutoff valves, and do they still close; if not, which units lose water when the riser is shut, and how much notice do they need; and are there set hours for plumbing work. A plumber who finds a seized valve at 8 a.m. on install day can turn a one-day job into a building-wide problem, so it pays to test the valves during the site visit.

The rest is logistics that boards care about more than the bathroom itself:

  • Getting the old tub out. A steel or cast-iron tub has to come down a stairwell or an elevator. Ask whether the elevator can be padded and reserved.
  • Parking and a dumpster. Many communities limit work trucks and do not allow a dumpster in the lot; ask where the crew can park and how debris leaves.
  • Noise hours. Tile removal and tub demolition are loud for an hour or two. Most rules allow it on weekdays during the day.
  • Moving a drain in a slab. In a ground-floor unit or a townhome on a slab, a curbless shower may need the concrete cut. In an upper unit, the floor below may be the neighbor's ceiling. Both usually need association sign-off because they touch common elements.

Those last two points are why many condo owners choose a low-threshold shower that fits the existing tub space and drain, rather than a fully curbless one. It removes the high tub wall without touching the slab. Paying for it

Is there help paying for an accessible condo bathroom?

Sometimes. Original Medicare generally does not cover walk-in tubs or shower conversions. Health First Colorado members on the EBD, BI, CMHS or CIH waivers may qualify for the Home Modification benefit, capped at $14,000 and approved through a case manager. Eligible veterans may qualify for VA HISA, up to $6,800 lifetime for a service-connected condition (or a 50% or higher rating) or $2,000 otherwise. Installed pricing depends on the bathroom and the model you choose, so every household gets a free, itemized written quote, which these programs and most HOA boards want to see anyway.
