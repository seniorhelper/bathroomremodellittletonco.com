What actually makes a bathroom safer?

The bathroom is where wet floors, hard surfaces and awkward movements meet. A CDC study of emergency visits found that 81.1% of nonfatal bathroom injuries were caused by falls, and about two-thirds happened in or near the tub or shower.

That points straight at the fixes that matter most. In rough order of impact for most people:

  1. Get rid of the step-over. A standard tub wall is the single hardest thing in most bathrooms. Replace it with a low-threshold or roll-in shower, or a walk-in tub with a door and seat.
  2. Grab bars where your hands actually go. By the shower entry, on the shower wall, and beside the toilet, anchored into studs or solid blocking. A towel bar is not a grab bar.
  3. Somewhere to sit. A built-in or fold-down shower seat, or the seat in a walk-in tub.
  4. A handheld shower on a slide bar, so you can wash seated and aim water where you need it.
  5. Slip resistance and light. A textured shower floor, a non-slip mat outside it, and bright, even lighting with a night light for 2 a.m. trips.
  6. Temperature control. A pressure-balancing or thermostatic valve, and a water heater set to 120°F as the CPSC recommends, to prevent scalds.

No bathing product eliminates the risk of falls; products are designed to reduce risk when installed and used properly.

Choose the core change

Local housing

How Littleton homes change the accessibility plan.

The same goal plays out differently in a 1920s cottage near Main Street than in a 1990s Highlands Ranch two-story. These are the accessibility questions we'd ask in each.

Older homes near downtown

Bathrooms tend to be small and doorways narrow, often too narrow for a walker to turn through easily. Old plaster and irregular framing make it worth confirming where solid wood is before choosing grab bar spots. A compact walk-in tub or a transfer-style shower that fits the original alcove is often a better answer than trying to make room for a full roll-in footprint.

Ranches and split-levels from the 1960s to 1980s

The classic hall bath with a tub-shower combo is the most straightforward place for a conversion, because the alcove is usually a standard size. The catch in split-levels is the stairs: if the only full bath is half a flight away from the bedroom, consider whether the main-level half bath could become the everyday bathroom instead. That's a bigger plumbing question, and an early one to ask.

Newer two-story homes

Primary baths are roomy, but they're often upstairs, and the big garden tub is the least-used and hardest-to-enter fixture in the house. Replacing it with a large roll-in or low-threshold shower frees floor space and adds a seat. If stairs are becoming a problem, plan the main-floor bathroom now, while it's a choice rather than an emergency.

Curbless needs the floor's cooperation

A true zero-threshold shower needs the floor to slope to the drain, which can mean recessing the subfloor between joists. Whether that's simple depends on joist direction, depth and what's below, so it's checked before quoting. A one-piece roll-in shower base with a very low threshold gets most of the benefit with far less structural work. The 2010 ADA Standards, a useful reference even though private homes don't have to follow them, limit roll-in thresholds to half an inch.

Paying for it

Help that may be available around Littleton.

None of these is guaranteed, and each has its own rules and waitlists. They're worth a phone call before you spend your own money.

  • Health First Colorado (Medicaid) waivers. The state's home modification benefit has a $14,000 cap on the BI, CMHS, EBD and CIH waivers, and covers bathroom modifications with case manager approval. Aging Safely Baths has a plain-English guide to Colorado Medicaid waiver bathroom modifications.
  • VA HISA for veterans. A lifetime benefit of up to $6,800 for a service-connected condition (or a veteran rated 50% or more service-connected), or up to $2,000 otherwise, for medically necessary changes such as roll-in showers. It doesn't cover spa or hot tub features. Start with your VA medical center's prosthetics office.
  • DRCOG Area Agency on Aging. The Denver Regional Council of Governments is the designated Area Agency on Aging for Arapahoe, Douglas and Jefferson counties, among others, and can point older adults to local services. See drcog.org.
  • Brothers Redevelopment. This Colorado nonprofit's Home Modification and Repair program serves low-income older adults and people with disabilities, installing items like grab bars, railings, ramps and accessible showers. Waitlists can be long; call 303-685-4225 to ask about your area.
  • Medicare. Original Medicare generally doesn't pay for walk-in tubs or bathroom remodels. Some Medicare Advantage plans offer bathroom safety benefits, so it's worth asking your plan.
  • Taxes. IRS Publication 502 lets medically necessary home improvements count as medical expenses, reduced by any increase in home value, and only above 7.5% of adjusted gross income. Ask a tax professional.
