Half the battle is knowing which building department you're dealing with. A "Littleton" address can be the City of Littleton, or unincorporated Arapahoe, Jefferson or Douglas County. Here's how to tell, what needs a permit, and how the inspections go.

By Zach Wennstedt, owner of Aging Safely Baths · Published October 25, 2026 · Facts checked October 25, 2026

Find your permit office Call 1-888-779-2284
The short answer

If your home is inside City of Littleton limits, permits go through the city's eTRAKiT portal, and the city requires one to alter or repair a building or to install, alter or replace plumbing, electrical, gas or mechanical systems, so most bathroom remodels beyond paint and accessories need one. Outside city limits, a Littleton mailing address usually means Arapahoe, Jefferson or Douglas County issues it instead. Contractors must be licensed with the city to work there.

Step one

Which office issues your permit?

The post office and the building department draw different maps. Plenty of homes with a Littleton mailing address sit outside the city entirely, so start by confirming your jurisdiction.

The quickest check: look up your address on your county assessor's website. The property record lists the taxing jurisdictions, and if a city is one of them, that city is almost always your building department. When in doubt, call the city's Permit Center and ask whether your address is theirs. It takes two minutes and saves a rejected application.

Step two

What needs a permit, and what doesn't?

The City of Littleton's own wording is broad. It requires building permits to construct, enlarge, alter, repair, move or demolish a building, and to install, alter, repair, convert or replace any electrical, gas, mechanical or plumbing system.

Step three

How a City of Littleton permit actually goes.

  1. Pick a licensed contractor. The city requires every contractor working within its jurisdiction to be currently licensed with the city, and it publishes a list you can check. Ask for the license before you sign.
  2. Apply through eTRAKiT. All permits are submitted through the city's eTRAKiT portal, where you can also upload plans, check status, pay and schedule inspections. Usually the contractor applies on your behalf.
  3. Describe the work clearly. A layout sketch showing fixture locations and what's changing makes review faster. Keep the scope on the permit matching the scope on your quote.
  4. Inspections at the right moments. Plumbing and electrical work is typically inspected before walls are closed, then again at final. Don't let anyone tile over work that hasn't been signed off.
  5. Keep the paperwork. Save the permit number and final inspection record with your house documents. Future buyers and their inspectors ask.

Can you pull the permit yourself?

Sometimes. The city offers "homeowner" permits only for residential properties, and only to homeowners who live in the home. If you're doing work in a single discipline, such as plumbing only, you don't need a general contractor but you do complete a Homeowner Affidavit. If you act as general contractor on a project spanning two or more disciplines, the city requires you to hire a City of Littleton licensed general contractor or show you can supervise the work by passing International Code Council testing. Most bathroom remodels touch plumbing and electrical, so for most people that means hiring a licensed contractor.

Why bother

Why permits matter more than the fee.

The fee is rarely the issue; it scales with the value of the work and is a small line on most bathroom quotes. What matters is what the permit gets you: a second set of trained eyes on the drain slope, the venting, the GFCI protection and the waterproofing details that disappear behind tile. When you sell, a buyer's inspector who spots a new bathroom will often ask whether it was permitted. "Yes, here's the final" ends that conversation. "No" can start a negotiation.

For accessibility work there's one more reason. Grab bar blocking, a curbless shower pan and a wider doorway are all easier to do right when the walls and floor are open, which is exactly when the inspector visits.
